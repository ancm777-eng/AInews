    NVIDIA Discloses Optics-on-Interposer Architecture: 256 Gb/s DWDM Directly on Silicon Interposers

    1. Executive Summary

    As frontier AI training clusters scale toward tens of thousands of accelerators, conventional Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) architectures encounter thermal and electrical channel loss bottlenecks caused by routing high-speed SerDes signals across lossy organic package substrates. Addressing this physical scaling limit, NVIDIA disclosed prototypes of its Optics-on-Interposer (OOI) technology featuring Dense Wavelength-Division Multiplexing (DWDM). By relocating the optical engine off the organic board directly onto the 2.5D silicon interposer alongside compute dies and HBM, the architecture reduces trace lengths from >20 mm to <2 mm. Utilizing an 8-wavelength DWDM link running at 32 Gb/s per lambda, OOI achieves a raw aggregate line throughput of 256 Gb/s per fiber strand while cutting I/O interface power by 60% to 70%.

    OOI (Optics-on-Interposer): Advanced packaging topology that co-locates photonic and electronic integrated circuits directly on a 2.5D silicon interposer adjacent to compute dies, eliminating lossy organic substrate traces.

    DWDM (Dense Wavelength-Division Multiplexing): Optical multiplexing technique combining multiple laser wavelengths onto a single optical fiber to scale aggregate data bandwidth without increasing fiber count.

    SerDes: Specialized high-speed physical-layer serializer/deserializer circuits converting parallel on-chip data buses into high-frequency serial streams for inter-chip transport.

    3. Strategic Outlook & Limitations

    Although OOI unlocks unprecedented edge-bandwidth density, integrating temperature-sensitive optical modulators and photodetectors onto the same silicon interposer as high-power compute dies creates steep thermo-mechanical reliability challenges. Thermal warpage and localized heat flux can detune precision DWDM resonance cavities, mandating advanced active thermal isolation. Furthermore, because on-interposer packaging drastically increases assembly complexity and prevents modular post-packaging component rework, any defect in an individual photonic die risks sacrificing the entire multi-die package, placing heavy reliance on advanced foundry yield optimization (such as TSMC's COUPE platform).

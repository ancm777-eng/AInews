    Kerr Self-Focusing Control for Depth-Precise 4H-SiC Femtosecond Laser Slicing

    1. Abstract

    An August 2026 arXiv preprint, 2608.03814, from Texas A&M University, the Korea Institute of Machinery and Materials, and the University of Science and Technology presents an extended Marburger-formula model for controlling Kerr self-focusing in infrared femtosecond laser stealth slicing of 4H-Silicon Carbide wafers. Laboratory-scale validation achieved depth-controlled internal modification at sub-100 μm targets, offering a predictive route to lower-stress wafer thinning for future 3D power electronics and vertically integrated power delivery networks.

    4H-SiC — A third-generation wide-bandgap semiconductor valued for high breakdown electric field and thermal conductivity.

    Kerr self-focusing — Intensity-driven refractive-index change that can focus a laser beam unpredictably inside a crystal.

    Extended Marburger formula — A self-focusing threshold model expanded here with normalized irradiance and focused ray optics.

    Stealth laser slicing — Non-contact creation of internal modified layers that enable subsequent wafer cleavage.

    2. Architecture & Performance

    Conventional mechanical diamond sawing of hard, brittle 4H-SiC can produce kerf loss, micro-cracks, and wafer breakage. The research instead maps laser pulse energy and focal depth to internal modification zones, using an infrared-wavelength femtosecond laser and an extended Marburger formula incorporating normalized irradiance and focused ray optics. The model predicts Kerr self-focusing thresholds and supports depth-precise modification before non-contact separation.

    3. Strategic Outlook & Limitations

    The preprint establishes a laboratory-scale optical framework rather than a production-ready process. Commercial 4H-SiC wafers vary in dopant concentration, crystal defects, and surface roughness, each of which can alter optical non-linearity and modification depth. Scaling to high-throughput manufacturing will therefore require real-time adaptive pulse calibration, robust yield characterization, and integration with advanced femtosecond laser equipment before the potential reductions in material loss and separation stress can be realized in 3D power-semiconductor packaging.
